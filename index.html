Bio

Stanley Sampa is a Zambian field ecologist, agroforester, GIS and remote sensing analyst, and community development practitioner based in Munyumbwe, Gwembe District. He trained as an agroforester at Copperbelt University, and working across farms and woodlands drew him to biodiversity and ecology, which he is now studying for an MSc in Tropical Ecology and Biodiversity Management. GIS and remote sensing tie the two together.

For ecology and conservation work, he runs camera-trap monitoring, vegetation surveys and tree inventories in the field, then turns biodiversity and forest data into ArcGIS Pro dashboards and StoryMaps. He uses techniques such as hexagonal spatial aggregation, land cover change detection and least-cost path modelling to show where habitats are changing and how landscapes connect.

He is District Facilitator with Nutri-Aid Trust on the NAT-ZGF CSSF Project, where he does community development through governance. He helps Farmer Field Schools and a Centre of Excellence renew their executive committees, review their constitutions, develop strategic and annual work plans and mobilise resources, alongside their climate-smart agriculture. He is also Training Manager at Tuskline Wildlife Research and Training Institute, where he has trained more than 100 students and 17 conservation professionals in camera traps, GIS and remote sensing, and Administrative Director of Know Your Wildlife and the Environment Zambia (KWEZI). Earlier, he supervised coffee nurseries and crop monitoring at Kateshi Estate in Kasama, worked as a PlantVillage field technician with ZARI in Kapiri Mposhi, and surveyed Miombo inventory plots in the Kasanka landscape.

His MSc research looks at how seasonal fire regimes shape canopy structure in Nsumbu National Park.

Case study · Sentinel-2 Dynamic World

Gwembe District, 2017 to 2026

Gwembe runs from the Zambezi escarpment down to the shores of Lake Kariba, and it is where I work with farmer groups. I compared Dynamic World land cover for 2017 and 2026 across the district’s 3,670 km², about 645 km² of it lake, and traced which classes turned into which.

Over nine years, 303 km² of tree cover became shrub and scrub while 168 km² went the other way. Overall, tree cover fell from 1,885 km² to 1,749 km². Built-up land roughly doubled, and most new settlement was built on former scrubland.

Least-cost path, Nsumbu National Park

Modelled the optimal route across the park for two periods. NDVI, land cover and slope from Landsat imagery were combined in a weighted overlay to build a cost surface, followed by cost distance and least-cost path analysis. Land cover was mapped into seven classes with supervised classification. As conditions changed between 2013 and 2026, the least-cost route grew from 15 km to 71.6 km, nearly five times longer.

The 2013 scene was captured in October, at the peak of the burning season, and the 2026 scene in June, so part of the difference in burnt area reflects timing.

Method
Weighted overlay, cost distance and least-cost path

Research

Fire, canopy and rainfall in Zambia’s landscapes

MSc · in progress Nsumbu National Park and Tondwa GMA

Seasonal fire regimes and 3D Miombo canopy structure

Combines NASA GEDI spaceborne LiDAR with Sentinel-2 burn severity, Sentinel-1 radar and VIIRS fire history to test how early and late dry-season fires change canopy height, plant area and above-ground biomass in Miombo woodland.

A ten-year fire regime baseline (2016–2026) is built in Google Earth Engine, and Random Forest models extend GEDI footprints into wall-to-wall canopy maps. The workflow runs end to end in R through rgee, terra, sf and ranger.

BSc thesis · 2022 Kasama District, Northern Province

Impact of inter-annual rainfall variability on vegetation cover and crop yield: a case study of Kasama District

My BSc special project at Copperbelt University tested how year-to-year changes in rainfall affect vegetation cover, land use and the yields of six crops across Kasama District in 2000, 2009 and 2020.

Methodology

  1. Downloaded cloud-free Landsat 4–7 TM/ETM+ and Landsat 8 OLI scenes from USGS for 2000, 2009 and 2020, and applied radiometric and geometric corrections.
  2. Classified land use and land cover with a Support Vector Machine into seven classes: farmland, forest, dense vegetation, water and wetland, less vegetation, bare land and built-up area.
  3. Checked each classification against Google Earth imagery at 180 random points, reporting overall, user’s and producer’s accuracy and the Kappa coefficient.
  4. Calculated NDVI, proportion of vegetation and land surface emissivity for each year with the raster calculator.
  5. Interpolated rainfall station records into rainfall surfaces with inverse distance weighting (IDW).
  6. Ran correlation and regression between mean annual rainfall, land cover classes, NDVI and the yields of maize, finger millet, groundnuts, beans, soya beans and cassava.
Software
ArcGIS 10.8 · QGIS 3.24 · Minitab · Excel

Now being developed into a multi-district follow-up with Sentinel-2, CHIRPS, MODIS, Google Earth Engine and Random Forest, designed to address the non-stationarity the pilot revealed.

Are Zambia’s protected areas holding their forest?

Are national parks, GMAs and forest reserves losing tree cover more slowly than the land around them?

Data
Dynamic World tree class 2017–2026 · Hansen Global Forest Change · park, GMA and forest reserve boundaries
Output
Map series and a dashboard ranking areas by inside-versus-outside loss

Methodology

  1. GEEBuild yearly dry-season tree cover and loss years, and total the loss and gain for every protected area.
  2. QGISClean the boundaries and draw 10 km buffers outside each area, leaving out neighbouring protected land.
  3. GEECompare annual loss inside each area with its buffer, and check a sample of change pixels against high-resolution imagery.
  4. ArcGIS ProProduce a map series and a dashboard ranking areas by inside-versus-outside loss.

Fire atlas of Zambia’s national parks

How much of each park burns each year, and how much burns late in the dry season, when fires are hottest?

Data
MODIS and VIIRS burned area · VIIRS active fire · Sentinel-2 dNBR
Output
Park statistics, fire-frequency maps and an ArcGIS StoryMap

Methodology

  1. GEEExtract burn dates for 2016–2026 and split them into early (May–July) and late (August–November) dry season.
  2. GEECalculate burned area, fire frequency and late-season share for each park.
  3. GEEMap Sentinel-2 burn severity for focus parks, with thresholds checked against MODIS and water masked out.
  4. QGISArcGIS ProBuild park statistics, fire-frequency maps and a StoryMap.

Biodiversity hexagons and survey gaps

Where are species records concentrated, where are the survey gaps, and which hotspots lie outside protected areas?

Data
Zambia biodiversity point data (RCMRD training) · GBIF records · protected area boundaries · RCoE geoportal layers
Output
Hexagon maps and a dashboard of hotspots and survey gaps

Methodology

  1. QGISRemove duplicates, imprecise coordinates and points outside Zambia.
  2. ArcGIS ProBuild 100 km² hexagons and count records and species in each.
  3. ArcGIS ProAdjust species counts for survey effort and flag hexagons with too few records as data gaps.
  4. ArcGIS ProOverlay protected areas to find rich hexagons outside them, and publish a dashboard.

Corridors between Kasanka, Lavushi Manda and Bangweulu

Can wildlife still move between the two parks and the Bangweulu Wetlands, and how has land-cover change since 2017 shifted the best routes?

Data
Dynamic World 2017 and 2026 · OpenStreetMap roads and settlements · 30 m DEM · protected area boundaries
Output
Corridor maps for 2017 and 2026 with a change summary

Methodology

  1. GEEExport land cover for both years across the landscape.
  2. QGISBuild a resistance surface from land cover, roads, settlements and slope.
  3. ArcGIS ProMap least-cost corridors between the three areas for each year with Linkage Mapper.
  4. ArcGIS ProCompare corridor length, cost and bottlenecks, and test how sensitive the results are to the resistance values.
